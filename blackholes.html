Black Holes.

Explore the anatomy of black holes and the major categories astronomers use to describe them. These extreme objects are regions of spacetime where gravity becomes extraordinarily strong.

What Is a Black Hole?

A black hole is a region of spacetime whose gravity is strong enough that, once something passes its event horizon, it cannot escape back out. Black holes are described by general relativity and are detected through their effects on nearby matter, light, and spacetime.

Anatomy of a Black Hole

1

Event Horizon

The event horizon is the boundary around a black hole beyond which escape to the outside universe is no longer possible.

2

Accretion Disk

A disk of hot gas and other material can form around some black holes. Friction and compression can make this material extremely hot and bright.

3

Photon Sphere

Around a non-rotating black hole, the photon sphere is a region where light can travel on unstable circular paths.

4

Singularity

Classical general relativity predicts a singularity at the centre of a black hole, where its equations indicate extremely extreme spacetime curvature. A complete theory of quantum gravity may be needed to describe the true physics there.

5

Relativistic Jets

Some actively feeding black holes can produce powerful jets of particles moving outward along directions near their rotational axes.

6

Black Hole Shadow

The shadow is a dark region produced by the way strong gravity bends and captures light around a black hole. It is larger than the event horizon itself.

Major Types of Black Holes

⭐ Stellar-Mass Black Holes

These black holes typically form when massive stars reach the end of their lives and their cores collapse. They generally have masses of several times that of the Sun to a few tens of solar masses.

Typical scale: ~3–100 solar masses

🌌 Intermediate-Mass Black Holes

Intermediate-mass black holes are expected to be heavier than typical stellar-mass black holes but much lighter than supermassive black holes. Their existence is an active area of astronomical research.

Approximate scale: ~100–100,000 solar masses

🌀 Supermassive Black Holes

These enormous black holes lie at the centres of most large galaxies. They can contain millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun.

Typical scale: millions–billions of solar masses

❓ Primordial Black Holes

Primordial black holes are hypothetical black holes that could have formed in the extremely early universe. No primordial black hole has been conclusively confirmed.

Status: Hypothetical

How Black Holes Can Form

01 — Massive star
A sufficiently massive star eventually exhausts the nuclear fuel that supports its core.
02 — Core collapse
The star's core can collapse under its own gravity after the star reaches the end of its evolutionary path.
03 — Compact object
Depending on the remaining core mass and other conditions, the collapse can produce a black hole.
04 — Growth
A black hole can grow by gaining mass from surrounding material or by merging with other black holes.

🔭 QENOAIX Fact

Black holes themselves do not shine like ordinary stars. Astronomers can nevertheless study them by observing nearby matter, gravitational effects, gravitational waves, and the way their strong gravity bends light.
